Propositional Logic

Propositions

propositional statement — a declarative sentence which is either true or false (also called a "proposition" or a "statement"); often represented with variables \(p\), \(q\), or \(r\)

  • a statement is atomic if it cannot be divided into smaller statements; otherwise it is molecular

What is NOT a propositional statement?

  • anything that is sometimes true and sometimes false (The letter "y" is a vowel.)

  • anything with unknowns or variables (\(x=3\))

  • incomplete statements (\(3+4\), The sum of two squares. )

  • questions, commands, etc

"p if and only if q"

You can think of the biconditional as a two-way conditional. So \(p\iff q\) is the same thing as saying \(p\implies q\) and \(q\implies p\). That is, \(p\) and \(q\) must both exist simultaneously, or they must both not exist simultaneously; you cannot have one without the other.

For example: "I like math if and only if I don't get a bad grade." I either: a) like math and get a good grade, or b) don't like math and get a bad grade. There is no in-between.

Logical Equivalences

Statements that have the same truth tables are logically equivalent, denoted with the symbol \(\equiv\). Here are some common/useful equivalencies:

  • De Morgan's Laws
    \(\neg(p\vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q \)
    \(\neg(p\wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q \)

  • Distributive Law
    \(p\wedge(q\vee r) \equiv (p \wedge q) \vee (p \wedge r) \)
    \(p\vee(q\wedge r) \equiv (p \vee q) \wedge (p \vee r) \)

  • Implications
    \((p\Rightarrow q) \equiv (\neg p \wedge q)\)
